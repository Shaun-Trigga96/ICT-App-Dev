Section E — Short Written Answers

Answer in your own words, then reveal the model answer and award yourself a mark. (4 × 5 = 20 marks)
5Distinguish between a research aim and research objectives, and give one ICT example of each.
Show model answer & self-mark

Aim = the single, overall thrust/purpose of the study (one broad statement of what you intend to investigate). Objectives = numbered, specific, single-focus steps that break the aim down, each starting with an action verb (to identify, to compare, to evaluate) and each mapping to a research question. Example — Aim: 'To investigate how ICT infrastructure deficits affect youth employment in SA townships.' Objective: 'To analyse the extent and nature of ICT infrastructure deficits in selected townships.'

5Explain what the Golden Thread is and why coherence between its parts matters.
Show model answer & self-mark

The Golden Thread is the logical alignment running through a study: title → problem statement → research questions → objectives → methodology/data → findings → conclusions. Coherence matters because each part must flow from and support the others, so the study actually answers what it set out to. A 'broken' thread (e.g. objectives that measure something the questions never asked, or a problem different from the title) makes the study inconsistent and unanswerable, and costs marks.

5Compare quantitative and qualitative research approaches in terms of purpose, type of data, and typical designs.
Show model answer & self-mark

Quantitative: aims to measure, test, compare and classify; uses numerical data; structured, fixed designs (cross-sectional, before-and-after, experimental); usually larger, often random samples; analysed statistically. Qualitative: aims to explore, understand and describe meanings, experiences and perceptions; uses words/text; flexible, emergent designs (case study, focus groups, interviews); smaller, purposive samples; analysed by themes. Mixed methods combine both.

5Outline the main steps of a systematic literature review and explain why it is called 'systematic'.
Show model answer & self-mark

Steps: (1) define a clear review question; (2) plan a search strategy — keywords/synonyms and databases; (3) apply inclusion and exclusion criteria; (4) screen records (identification → screening → eligibility → included, often via a PRISMA flow diagram); (5) extract consistent data from each study into a table (data extraction); (6) synthesise findings by grouping them into themes and comparing them. It is 'systematic' because the whole process — searching, screening, extraction and synthesis — is documented, transparent and repeatable, reducing bias.
