# Hamidullah Khan, PhD — 10 Research Prompts

## 1. Turn a broad topic into a feasible research question
I’m planning a [degree level] research project in [discipline] about [broad topic]. My setting is [location/context], I can access [participants/data/materials], and I have [time available]. Propose 3 focused research questions. For each, specify the population or material studied, key concepts or variables, suitable study design, data needed, and biggest feasibility issue. Avoid questions that require resources I haven’t listed. Recommend the most achievable question, explain the trade-off, and turn it into a working title, one research aim, and 3 aligned objectives. Add hypotheses only if appropriate. 

---

## 2. Build a literature search you can actually run
My research question is [question]. I will search [database names], focusing on [date range], [languages], and [population/context]. Break the question into searchable concept groups. Create a table of keywords, synonyms, spelling variants, and relevant subject headings where supported. Then produce two copy-ready search strings for each database: one broad and one focused. Adapt the syntax to the database and flag anything requiring verification. Explain how to adjust the search if results are too broad or too limited. Finish with clear inclusion/exclusion criteria and a search-log template. Do not invent articles or search results. 

---

## 3. Extract useful evidence from research papers
Using only the [papers/abstracts] I provide below, build an evidence matrix for this question: [research question]. Include: author/year, study aim, country/context, design, sample, data collection, analysis method, main findings, limitations, and relevance to my question. Preserve reported numbers and distinguish authors’ conclusions from your interpretation. Write “not reported” when information is missing. If only an abstract is available, label the extraction “abstract only” and avoid judging details that require the full paper. End with 3 agreements, 3 differences, and important evidence missing from this collection. Materials: [attach papers or paste text]. 

---

## 4. Write a literature review that synthesizes evidence
Write a [word count]-word thematic literature review addressing [research question], using only my supplied evidence matrix and source excerpts: [paste/attach]. First identify 3–5 themes. Organize the review around these themes, comparing findings, methods, populations, and study limitations. Explain possible reasons for conflicting results only when the supplied evidence supports them. Use [citation style] and connect each substantive claim to its supporting source. Avoid one paragraph per paper and unsupported claims of consensus. End with what the evidence establishes, what remains uncertain, and how my proposed study could contribute. Flag missing support as [source needed]. 

---

## 5. Find a defensible research gap
Examine this literature review/evidence matrix: [paste/attach]. My proposed topic is [topic], and my practical constraints are [time, access, skills, resources]. Identify 4 potential gaps. For each, show the supporting studies, what is unresolved, why resolving it matters, and a feasible research question and study design. Distinguish gaps demonstrated within these sources from claims about the whole field. Do not call something a gap simply because few studies appear in my collection. Rank the gaps by evidence strength, significance, and feasibility. For the strongest option, draft a 120-word rationale and list searches needed to check its originality. 

---

## 6. Design a methodology aligned with your question
Develop a methodology plan for [research question] and these objectives: [objectives]. My context is [setting], available data/participants are [details], and constraints are [time, budget, access]. Compare the two most suitable designs and recommend one with a clear justification. Specify sampling, recruitment, sample-size justification, data collection, key variables or concepts, analysis, ethics, and quality checks appropriate to the design. Include a table linking each objective to its data source, collection method, and analysis. State assumptions and limitations. If sample size requires a calculation, identify the inputs needed rather than inventing a number. 

---

## 7. Create a research instrument with a clear purpose
Create a [survey/interview guide] for my study on [topic], addressing [research objectives] with [participant group]. Draft [number] questions in language suitable for these participants. Map every question to an objective and explain what information it will collect. For a survey, include response options and scoring where appropriate; for interviews, include neutral follow-up probes. Check for leading wording, double questions, unclear terms, and unnecessary sensitive questions. Add a brief introduction and a pilot-testing checklist. Do not describe newly generated questions as validated. If I provide an existing instrument, distinguish its original items from proposed changes. Existing instrument, if any: [paste/none]. 

---

## 8. Choose and carry out an appropriate data analysis
Help me analyze [quantitative/qualitative] data for [research question] using [Excel/SPSS/R/Python/qualitative analysis software]. I’m providing [dataset/transcripts], [variable definitions/interview guide], and [study design]. First check data structure, missing information, and alignment with my objectives. Propose an analysis plan linking each objective to the relevant variables or text, method, assumptions or quality checks, and expected output. Then give numbered instructions suitable for a beginner. If you can execute the analysis, show the code or steps and actual outputs; otherwise, provide runnable instructions without inventing results. Explain findings in plain language and flag interpretations the design cannot support. 

---

## 9. Turn your findings into results and discussion sections
Using my research question [question], methods [summary], analysis outputs [paste/attach], and literature [provided sources], draft separate Results and Discussion sections totaling [word count] words. In Results, organize findings by objective and report only supplied evidence. For quantitative findings, preserve estimates, units, uncertainty, and sample sizes where available. For qualitative findings, support themes with supplied participant quotations. In Discussion, explain how the findings answer the question, compare them with the provided literature, and discuss implications, limitations, and alternative explanations. Do not invent statistics, quotations, or citations. Flag inconsistencies and missing information before drafting. 

---

## 10. Assemble a coherent research paper from your materials
Help assemble my research paper for [journal/course] using its requirements [paste] and my materials [attach notes, section drafts, tables, and references]. Target length: [word count]. Citation style: [style]. First create a section outline with word allocations. Then organize and edit my material into the required structure, keeping the research question, objectives, methods, results, and conclusions consistent. Use [information needed] wherever essential content is missing. Do not invent procedures, findings, or references. Write the abstract from the assembled manuscript. Finish with a short revision checklist identifying unsupported claims, contradictions, missing methodological details, and citation/reference mismatches.
